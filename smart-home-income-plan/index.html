What is a smart home income plan? UK inheritance tax explained

By Steve Hunt ACII TEP · Published 29 October 2026 · Last reviewed 29 October 2026 · Video 18 min · Full transcript below

The short answer

A smart home income plan is a home income plan with a dial. A lifetime mortgage buys a purchased life annuity, and each year the interest is either paid or left to roll up, to hold the estate just under £2 million. In the UK, £2 million is the line where the residence nil rate band starts to be taken away. Steve Hunt calls it HIP 3.0. Smart home income plan is his own name for the idea: it uses products you can buy today, but it is not a product in its own right.

Between £2 million and £2.7 million, every extra £1 in a widow's estate costs 60p in tax on 2026/27 rules, so every £1 the plan brings the estate down saves 60p. But the family still inherits less, because the money has been spent. In Steve's UK worked example, Mrs Miggins, 85, sets her dial at £1.95 million. The mansion tax part covers England only: the High Value Council Tax Surcharge and its deferral scheme were confirmed on 28 October 2026.

What were HIP 1.0 and HIP 2.0, and what does HIP 3.0 add?

HIP 3.0 adds a dial to a plan that is more than 50 years old. The first home income plans were sold in the 1970s: you took a mortgage on your house and bought an annuity with the money, the annuity paid the interest, and the rest was yours to spend. Call that HIP 1.0. In the late 1980s the money went into investment bonds instead of annuities. The bonds fell, the interest rolled up, and many older people were badly hurt. HIP 2.0, the subject of Steve's last video, is a lifetime mortgage that buys a purchased life annuity: a guaranteed income for life, bought with your own money, with no investment bonds and today's Equity Release Council protections. HIP 3.0, the smart home income plan, adds the dial.

Why does £2 million matter for inheritance tax?

Above £2 million, the residence nil rate band is taken away at £1 for every £2, under section 8D of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984. The residence nil rate band is the extra tax-free amount for a home left to children or grandchildren: £175,000 each, and a widow can also claim her late husband's unused band, so up to £350,000. With the ordinary nil rate band of £325,000 each, a widow can leave up to £1 million free of inheritance tax. These bands are frozen until April 2031. From 6 April 2027, most unused pension funds also count towards the £2 million, under the Finance Act 2026. While the band is being taken away, every extra £1 in the estate costs 60p in tax: 40p of inheritance tax, plus 20p for the lost band. With the full £350,000 band, it is all gone once the estate reaches £2.7 million.

How does the smart home income plan dial work?

Mrs Miggins sets her dial to £1.95 million, just under the £2 million line, like the dial on a thermostat. Her estate is her house, plus her savings, minus her lifetime mortgage. After the £500,000 lifetime mortgage in Steve's last video, that came to about £2.1 million, so she starts about £150,000 too hot. When house prices go up, her estate runs hot, so she lets the interest roll up: the debt grows, and her estate cools down. When house prices fall, her estate runs cold, so she pays the interest, and perhaps some of the loan, out of her annuity income, by spending less that year. Paying it from her savings would not help, because her savings and her debt would go down together. Once a year, with a fresh valuation, she and her adviser check the dial. It needs a lifetime mortgage that lets her pay in some years and not in others: since March 2022 the Equity Release Council's standards have given new lifetime mortgages the right to make penalty-free payments, subject to the lender's rules.

Sources Published source: Equity Release Council: what happens if I want to repay the loan early? · Steve's analysis: The smart home income plan dial: Steve's arithmetic on the stated assumptions

Why set the dial at £1.95 million and not £2 million?

Because the tax is not fair in both directions. If her estate runs £100,000 too cold, below £2 million, her family loses £60,000, not the full £100,000, because £40,000 of it would have gone in inheritance tax anyway. If her estate runs £100,000 too hot, above £2 million, her family keeps only £40,000: inheritance tax takes £40,000 and the lost residence nil rate band takes another £20,000. So the dial is set on the cool side, with £50,000 of room for a surveyor's rounding.

Sources Law: Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.8D · Steve's analysis: The smart home income plan dial: Steve's arithmetic on the stated assumptions

How much can rolling up the interest cool the estate?

Only by the amount of interest added, so the size of the mortgage matters. On a £500,000 lifetime mortgage at an assumed fixed rate of 6% a year, rolling up adds about £30,000 of debt in the first year. If a £2.5 million house goes up by 2.5% that year, it gains £62,500, so the dial on its own cannot keep up. She can then take a further advance on her lifetime mortgage and buy a second annuity. Money spent on an annuity that stops when she dies leaves her estate the day it is spent. If the annuity has a guarantee period, any payments still due after she dies do count in her estate. The lender has to agree, there are fees, and she has to spend the extra income: if it sits in the bank, the estate warms up again.

Sources HMRC: HMRC Inheritance Tax Manual, IHTM20634 (annuities which continue after the death) · Steve's analysis: The smart home income plan dial: Steve's arithmetic on the stated assumptions

Does a smart home income plan work for a smaller house?

Yes, and it can work even better. Take a £1.5 million house and set the dial at £950,000, just under the £1 million a widow can leave free of inheritance tax with both sets of allowances. Keep the estate there, and the family pays no inheritance tax at all. The catch: below £1 million there is no tax to share a fall in house prices, so a cold snap costs the family the full amount, and paying the interest matters more.

What is the mansion tax, and can it be deferred?

The mansion tax is the government's High Value Council Tax Surcharge. From April 2028, owners of homes in England worth £2 million or more, valued at April 2026, will pay a yearly charge on top of their council tax: £2,500 from £2 million, £3,500 from £2.5 million, £5,000 from £3.5 million and £7,500 from £5 million, going up each year with inflation from 2029-30. It is charged on the value of the house, with nothing taken off for a mortgage, so a smart home income plan can bring her estate under £2 million for inheritance tax and she still pays the surcharge on her £2.5 million house. On 28 October 2026 the government confirmed that an owner can defer it if household income is £35,000 or less, or savings are £16,000 or less, or someone in the household meets the disability rules. Interest is added, and the bill is paid when the house changes hands. The law will be in the Finance Bill 2026-27, and the rate of interest has not been set yet.

Sources Published source: GOV.UK: High Value Council Tax Surcharge, updated 28 October 2026 (bands, April 2026 valuation, inflation uprating from 2029-30, deferral); HM Treasury: Budget 2026, Overview of Tax Legislation and Rates, 28 October 2026, paragraph 1.22 (High Value Council Tax Surcharge, Finance Bill 2026-27, from April 2028)

Is deferring the mansion tax better than a smart home income plan?

Deferring the mansion tax only puts the bill off, with interest. Ten years at £3,500 is £35,000 before interest and inflation, taken out of the house when she dies, and to qualify she has to keep her income or her savings low. With a smart home income plan, her annuity income is well over £35,000 and her savings are over £16,000, so she would not qualify to defer: she pays the surcharge out of her income instead, and spends the rest. Both plans build up a debt against her house. Only one gives her an income to live on now.

How could an 85-year-old spend the income?

The income has to be spent, or given away, for the plan to work: if she saves it, it goes back into her estate. In Steve's last video, £500,000 bought Mrs Miggins a purchased life annuity of £76,747.44 a year, in an illustration dated 12 October 2026: £6,356.13 a month after the tax deducted, on 2026/27 rules. Budget 2026 set the basic rate on savings income at 24% from 6 April 2027, so the tax on the taxable part goes up by about £8 a month. Be careful with giving it away: most of each payment is her own capital coming back, and under section 21(3) of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984 that part is not income for the normal expenditure out of income exemption, so gifts of it come under the seven year rule. At 85, living alone in a big house, many people need some live-in care, which costs about £1,200 to £1,750 a week through an agency in 2026. The care can come from the family: pay for real care at the going rate is not a gift for inheritance tax, under section 10 of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984. It is taxable income for the carers, and they can pay some of it into a pension. It has to be real work, written down, at the going rate, and paid by bank transfer. Paying family carers can make her an employer, with payroll, National Insurance and pension duties, and pension money is locked away until 57 from April 2028.

What are the risks, and who does a smart home income plan not suit?

Not every lifetime mortgage lets you pay in some years and not others, and the plan only works with one that does. After the 30 day cancellation period, the annuity cannot be cashed in. The income is level, and prices will rise. In a year when the interest rolls up, the debt grows, with interest on interest, and in England the home income plan rule for care fees only ignores interest that is actually paid: the Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, Schedule 9, paragraph 17, carried across by England's care charging regulations of 2014. It needs an adviser, a fresh valuation and an honest look at the dial every year: set and forget will not do. It does not suit anyone in poor health, because purchased life annuities are not offered on impaired life terms; younger people, because the annuity pays less; or anyone who would rather downsize. The lifetime mortgage's no negative equity guarantee means the estate can never owe more than the house sells for.

So, can a smart home income plan cut inheritance tax?

Yes. For an estate a little over £2 million, every £1 it brings the estate down saves 60p in tax, on 2026/27 rules. But the family still inherits less, because the money has been spent. With the mansion tax deferral, she qualifies by staying poor, the government sets the interest rate, and the bill lands when the house changes hands. With a smart home income plan, she sets the temperature, she checks it once a year with her adviser, and she spends the income now. Tax rules can change, and how they apply depends on your own circumstances. This is education only. It is not advice.

Who called inheritance tax a voluntary levy paid by those who distrust their heirs?

Roy Jenkins, in the House of Commons on 19 March 1986, in the debate on the 1986 Budget. That Budget took most lifetime gifts out of capital transfer tax, which he saw as a return to death duties. Of that tax he said: "If people die old, it is, broadly speaking, a voluntary levy paid by those who distrust their heirs more than they dislike the Inland Revenue." The words are in Hansard, volume 94, column 325. The Finance Act 1986 renamed capital transfer tax as inheritance tax.

0:00What is a smart home income plan?

Hi there. Today's question is about smart home income plans. Can they cut your inheritance tax bill? How do they work? And what's the catch? I'm Steve Hunt. I'm a Chartered Insurance Risk Manager and a Trust and Estate Practitioner. And I've been in the industry since 1980. And I'm here to answer the questions that you ask.

0:28The short answer

The short answer. A smart home income plan is a home income plan with a dial. A lifetime mortgage buys a purchased life annuity. Each year the interest is either paid or left to roll up, to hold the estate just under £2 million. In the UK, £2 million is the line where the residence nil rate band starts to be taken away.

I'm Steve Hunt, and smart home income plan is my own name for this idea. I also call it HIP 3.0. It uses products you can buy today, but it is not a product in its own right. This example uses UK tax rules for 2026/27. The mansion tax part covers England only. The government confirmed it, and its deferral scheme, on 28 October 2026.

1:27What were HIP 1.0 and HIP 2.0, and what does HIP 3.0 add?

What were HIP 1.0 and HIP 2.0, and what does HIP 3.0 add? HIP 3.0 adds a dial to a plan that is more than fifty years old. The first home income plans were sold in the 1970s. You took a mortgage on your house and bought an annuity with the money. The annuity paid the interest, and the rest was yours to spend. Call that HIP 1.0.

In the late 1980s the idea was hijacked. The money went into investment bonds instead of annuities. The bonds fell, the interest rolled up, and many older people were badly hurt. The name "home income plan" has been mud ever since. HIP 2.0 was the subject of my last video. A lifetime mortgage, the most common kind of equity release, buys a purchased life annuity.

A purchased life annuity is a guaranteed income for life, bought with your own money. There are no investment bonds, and the lifetime mortgage comes with today's Equity Release Council protections. HIP 3.0, the smart home income plan, adds the dial. My dad always said evolution is far better than revolution. He was right.

2:52Why does £2 million matter for inheritance tax?

Why does £2 million matter for inheritance tax? Above £2 million, the residence nil rate band is taken away at £1 for every £2. That is section 8D of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984. The residence nil rate band is the extra tax-free amount for a home left to children or grandchildren. It is £175,000 each. A widow can also claim her late husband's unused band, so she can have up to £350,000.

Add the ordinary nil rate band of £325,000 each, and a widow can leave up to £1 million free of inheritance tax. These bands are frozen until April 2031. From 6 April 2027, most unused pension funds also count towards the £2 million, under the Finance Act 2026. While the band is being taken away, every extra £1 in the estate costs 60p in tax.

That is 40p of inheritance tax, plus 20p for the lost band. With the full £350,000, the band is all gone once the estate reaches £2.7 million. So for an estate a little over £2 million, the first job is to get it back under the line.

4:29How does the smart home income plan dial work?

How does the smart home income plan dial work? Mrs Miggins sets her dial to £1.95 million, just under the £2 million line. Think of it as the dial on the thermostat on the wall. Mrs Miggins is made up, but her numbers are real. She is 85, a widow in good health, in the family home in England. That £1.95 million is the temperature she aims for.

Her estate is her house, plus her savings, minus her lifetime mortgage. In my last video, after her £500,000 lifetime mortgage, that came to about £2.1 million. So she starts about £150,000 too hot. When house prices go up, her estate runs hot. So she lets the interest roll up. The debt grows, and her estate cools down. When house prices fall, her estate runs cold.

So she pays the interest, and perhaps some of the loan, out of her annuity income. The debt shrinks, and her estate warms back up. She pays it from her annuity income, by spending less that year. Paying it from her savings would not help, because her savings and her debt would go down together. Once a year, with a fresh valuation, she and her adviser check the dial.

That is the smart part: smart management of the debt and the estate. This needs a lifetime mortgage that lets her pay in some years and not in others. Since March 2022, the Equity Release Council's standards have given new lifetime mortgages the right to make penalty-free payments, subject to the lender's rules.

6:26Why set the dial at £1.95 million, not £2 million?

Why set the dial at £1.95 million and not £2 million? Because the tax is not fair in both directions. If her estate runs £100,000 too cold, below £2 million, her family loses £60,000, not the full £100,000. £40,000 of it would have gone in inheritance tax anyway. The taxman shares 40p of every pound lost. If her estate runs £100,000 too hot, above £2 million, her family keeps only £40,000.

Inheritance tax takes £40,000, and the lost residence nil rate band takes another £20,000. The taxman takes 60p of every pound gained. So the dial is set on the cool side, with £50,000 of room for a surveyor's rounding.

7:28How much can rolling up the interest cool the estate?

How much can rolling up the interest cool the estate? Rolling up the interest only cools the estate by the amount of interest added. So the size of the mortgage matters. In my last video, Mrs Miggins took a lifetime mortgage of £500,000. At an assumed fixed rate of 6% a year, rolling up adds about £30,000 of debt in the first year.

If her £2.5 million house goes up by 2.5% that year, it gains £62,500. The dial on its own cannot keep up. Then she can take a further advance on her lifetime mortgage and buy a second annuity. Money spent on an annuity that stops when she dies leaves her estate the day it is spent, so the room cools straight away.

If the annuity has a guarantee period, any payments still due after she dies do count in her estate. The lender has to agree, there are fees, and she has to spend the extra income. If it sits in the bank, the room warms up again.

8:44Does a smart home income plan work for a smaller house?

Does a smart home income plan work for a smaller house? Yes, and here it can be even better. Take a £1.5 million house. Set the dial at £950,000, just under the £1 million a widow can leave free of inheritance tax with both sets of allowances. Keep the estate there, and the family pays no inheritance tax at all. There is one catch.

Below £1 million there is no tax to share a fall in house prices, so a cold snap costs the family the full amount. At this level, paying the interest matters more.

9:28What is the mansion tax, and can it be deferred?

What is the mansion tax, and can it be deferred? The mansion tax is the government's High Value Council Tax Surcharge. From April 2028, owners of homes in England worth £2 million or more, valued at April 2026, will pay a yearly charge on top of their council tax. It is £2,500 from £2 million, £3,500 from £2.5 million, £5,000 from £3.5 million, and £7,500 from £5 million.

It will go up each year with inflation from 2029/30. It is charged on the value of the house, with nothing taken off for a mortgage. So a smart home income plan can bring her estate under £2 million for inheritance tax, and she still pays the surcharge on her £2.5 million house.

On 28 October 2026 the government confirmed that an owner can defer it if household income is £35,000 or less, or savings are £16,000 or less, or someone in the household meets the disability rules. Interest is added, and the bill is paid when the house changes hands. In all but name, that is a roll-up lifetime mortgage run by the government. The government will legislate for it in the Finance Bill 2026-27.

The rate of interest has not been set yet.

11:19Is deferring the mansion tax better than a smart home income plan?

Is deferring the mansion tax better than a smart home income plan? Deferring the mansion tax only puts the bill off, with interest. Ten years at £3,500 is £35,000 before interest and inflation, taken out of the house when she dies. And to qualify, she has to keep her income or her savings low. All she gets for it is a cold winter.

With a smart home income plan, her annuity income is well over £35,000 and her savings are over £16,000, so she would not qualify to defer. She pays the surcharge out of her income instead, and spends the rest. Both plans build up a debt against her house. Only one gives her an income to live on now.

12:15How could an 85-year-old spend the income?

How could an 85-year-old spend the income? The income has to be spent, or given away, for the plan to work. If she saves it, it goes back into her estate and the dial does nothing. In my last video, £500,000 bought Mrs Miggins a purchased life annuity of £76,747.44 a year, in an illustration dated 12 October 2026. After the tax deducted, that is £6,356.13 a month, on 2026/27 rules.

From 6 April 2027, the basic rate on savings income goes up to 24%, so the tax on the taxable part goes up by about £8 a month. In a year when the interest rolls up, all of it is hers to spend. Be careful with giving it away. Most of each payment is her own capital coming back.

Under section 21(3) of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984, that part is not income for the normal expenditure out of income exemption. Gifts of it come under the seven year rule. At 85, living alone in a big house, many people need some live-in care. Agency live-in care costs about £1,200 to £1,750 a week in 2026. The care can come from the family.

Mrs Miggins pays her three granddaughters to take turns living with her and looking after her. Pay for real care at the going rate is not a gift for inheritance tax, under section 10 of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984. It is taxable income for the granddaughters, and they can pay some of it into a pension and get tax relief. The honest bits.

It has to be real work, written down, at the going rate, and paid by bank transfer. Pay far above the going rate, and the extra is a gift. Paying them can also make Mrs Miggins an employer, with payroll, National Insurance and pension duties. Pension money is locked away until age 57 from April 2028. Keep the paperwork.

14:51What are the risks, and who does it not suit?

What are the risks, and who does a smart home income plan not suit? Not every lifetime mortgage lets you pay in some years and not others. The plan only works with one that does. After the 30 day cancellation period, the annuity cannot be cashed in. The income is level, and prices will rise. In a year when the interest rolls up, the debt grows, with interest on interest.

In England, the home income plan rule for care fees only ignores interest that is actually paid, so in those years it does not help. That rule is in the Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, carried across by England's care charging regulations of 2014. It needs an adviser, a fresh valuation and an honest look at the dial every year. Set and forget will not do.

It does not suit anyone in poor health, because purchased life annuities are not offered on impaired life terms. It does not suit younger people, because the annuity pays less. And it does not suit anyone who would rather downsize. The lifetime mortgage's no negative equity guarantee means the estate can never owe more than the house sells for.

16:17So, can a smart home income plan cut inheritance tax?

So, can a smart home income plan cut inheritance tax? Yes. For an estate a little over £2 million, every £1 it brings the estate down saves 60p in tax, on 2026/27 rules. But the family still inherits less, because the money has been spent. With the mansion tax deferral, she qualifies by staying poor, the government sets the interest rate, and the bill lands when the house changes hands.

With a smart home income plan, she sets the temperature, she checks it once a year with her adviser, and she spends the income now. Tax rules can change, and how they apply depends on your own circumstances. This is education only. It is not advice.

And as always, I leave you now with those famous comments from Roy Jenkins, as true now as they were in 1986 when he first spoke them in the House of Commons when talking about inheritance tax, where he said it was a voluntary levy paid by those who distrust their heirs more than they dislike the Inland Revenue.
