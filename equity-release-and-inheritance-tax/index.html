Can equity release reduce inheritance tax? UK worked example

By Steve Hunt ACII TEP · Published 24 October 2026 · Last reviewed 24 October 2026 · Video 16 min · Full transcript below

The short answer

On its own, equity release does little for inheritance tax. If you borrow money and leave it in the bank, your estate stays the same size. But if the money buys a purchased life annuity, the sums change. In Steve's UK worked example, Mrs Miggins, 85, takes a £500,000 lifetime mortgage on her £2.5 million home, and the money buys a purchased life annuity of £76,747.44 a year. Steve calls this HIP 2.0: the original home income plan, with today's protections.

On the assumptions in the video, her inheritance tax falls from £760,000 to £460,000, which is £300,000 less. But her family still inherit about £200,000 less, because the £500,000 has gone to the annuity provider. The tax saving is a side effect. The main point is the income: if she pays the 6% interest out of the annuity, she has £3,856.13 a month left to live on, to start with, and the debt stays at £500,000.

What is a lifetime mortgage, and how can it buy an annuity?

Equity release lets you take money out of your home without having to move. The most common kind in the UK is a lifetime mortgage: you borrow against your home, and the loan is usually repaid when the last borrower dies, or moves into long-term care for good. In HIP 2.0, the money from the lifetime mortgage buys a purchased life annuity, which turns a lump sum into a guaranteed income, for life. It is bought with your own capital, so it is a different thing from a pension annuity, which is bought with pension money.

Sources Published source: Equity Release Council: professional standards and guarantees

How is HIP 2.0 different from the home income plans mis-sold in the 1980s?

HIP 2.0 has no investment bond, and its income does not depend on the stock market. In the late 1980s, people borrowed against their homes and the money went into investment bonds. Interest rates rose, the bonds did not deliver, and some people lost the roof over their heads. In the early 1990s the regulators effectively stopped the sale of those bond plans, and in December 1991 the industry set up Safe Home Income Plans, SHIP, which became the Equity Release Council in 2012. The original home income plan was much simpler: borrow against the home, buy an annuity, pay the interest out of the annuity, and live on what is left. On 8 July 1992 the Treasury minister Anthony Nelson told the House of Commons it was "the only sort of scheme to receive encouragement through the tax system". That was the tax position in 1992. The old tax relief on the interest is not available for new loans today.

What could £500,000 pay an 85-year-old?

In an illustration from a UK annuity provider, dated 12 October 2026, £500,000 bought a woman aged 85 a purchased life annuity of £76,747.44 a year. That is £6,395.62 a month, level, single life, paid monthly, with payments guaranteed for at least one year. Most of each payment is her own money coming back to her, plus the insurer's promise to keep paying for as long as she lives. Rates change, and this is an illustration, not a personal quote.

Sources Provider evidence: Aviva Immediate Life Annuity illustration, 12 October 2026: £500,000, woman aged 85, single life, level, monthly in arrears, payments for at least a year, guaranteed to 21 November 2026

How is purchased life annuity income taxed?

Only a small part of each payment is taxed. Most of it counts as her own capital coming back, which is tax free under sections 717 to 724 of the Income Tax (Trading and Other Income) Act 2005. On the illustration, £6,198.17 of each monthly payment is tax-free capital, and £197.45 is taxed as savings income. The provider deducts 20% from that taxable slice, which is £39.49, and pays her £6,356.13 a month. That is the tax deducted, which may not be her final tax bill. With very little other income, she may be able to claim some or all of it back. Budget 2026, on 28 October 2026, set the basic rate on savings income at 24% from 6 April 2027. If all of the taxable slice is taxed at that rate, that is about £8 a month more.

Should she pay the lifetime mortgage interest, or let it roll up?

Paying the interest keeps the debt where it is. Letting it roll up gives her more to spend now, but the debt grows. At an assumed fixed rate of 6% a year, the interest on £500,000 is £2,500 a month. If she pays it every month out of the annuity, she has £3,856.13 a month left to start with, and the debt stays at £500,000. The mortgage must allow her to pay the interest this way. If she lets it roll up, she keeps the whole £6,356.13 a month, but the debt grows: with the interest added yearly, it is about £670,000 after five years, and about £895,000 after ten. Compound interest works against you here. The younger you are, the longer it has to grow, and the more paying the interest matters.

Who might HIP 2.0 not suit?

HIP 2.0 is a niche plan, and four groups of people should think hard before going near it. First, anyone who would rather downsize: if downsizing is an option, it should be considered first. Second, anyone in poor health: purchased life annuities are not offered on impaired life terms, so they do not pay more for poor health. The one niche exception is a care fees annuity, which is bought to pay for care. Third, younger people: the younger you are, the less the annuity pays, so there is far less left once the interest is paid. Fourth, anyone worried about dying early: the annuity stops, apart from anything left of the guarantee, but the loan still has to be repaid from the house. Extra income can also reduce means-tested benefits, such as Pension Credit.

Sources Law: State Pension Credit Act 2002, s.15(1)(d) (income from annuity contracts counts as income) · Steve's experience: Steve's market knowledge as at 12 October 2026

What are the risks and costs of HIP 2.0?

The plan cannot be undone. After the 30 day cancellation period, the annuity cannot be cashed in. The mortgage and the annuity are two separate contracts, so cancelling the annuity does not cancel the mortgage. The income is level, and prices will rise: at 2.5% inflation a year, in ten years, £3,856 a month will only buy what about £3,000 buys today. It costs money to set up: valuation, legal, advice and arrangement fees. And the mortgage rate matters: every quarter of one percent on £500,000 is about £104 a month.

How do care fees work with a purchased life annuity in England?

In England, when the council works out what you pay towards your care, annuity income usually counts in full. But if the annuity was bought with a loan secured on your home, and you pay the interest, the part of the annuity income that pays the interest can be ignored. The rule is in the Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, and England's care charging regulations of 2014 carry it across. The conditions are strict. At least 90% of the loan must buy an annuity for life. The interest must actually be paid, so interest that rolls up does not count. When the loan was made, you, and any joint annuitant, must have been at least 65, or State Pension age if that was higher. And you, or another annuitant, must still live in the home. Whether a plan qualifies depends on the exact terms of the mortgage and the annuity. Mrs Miggins's £100,000 of savings is over England's £23,250 upper capital limit, so she would pay her own care fees at first anyway. If she moves into a care home for good, the rule stops, and the loan is usually repaid from the sale of the house. The annuity carries on paying, towards her fees.

Sources Law: Care and Support (Charging and Assessment of Resources) Regulations 2014, reg. 16; Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, Sch. 9, para. 17; Care and Support (Charging and Assessment of Resources) Regulations 2014, Sch. 1, para. 20 · Published source: Department of Health and Social Care: social care charging for care and support 2026 to 2027, local authority circular (upper capital limit £23,250)

What protections apply to the lifetime mortgage and the annuity?

Lifetime mortgages that meet the Equity Release Council's standards come with four key protections: a fixed rate, or a capped rate, for the life of the loan; the right to live in your home for life, as long as it stays your main home and you keep to the loan terms; the right to move to another suitable home; and a no negative equity guarantee, so when the house is sold properly, the estate can never owe more than the sale proceeds. Independent legal advice is required before signing. For the annuity, if an eligible insurer fails, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme protects 100% of the annuity, with no upper limit.

Sources Published source: Equity Release Council: professional standards and guarantees; Financial Services Compensation Scheme: what we cover (long-term insurance 100% protected)

How could HIP 2.0 reduce inheritance tax by £300,000?

On the assumptions in the video, inheritance tax falls from £760,000 to £460,000. That is £300,000 less: £200,000 comes from the smaller estate, and £100,000 from getting back part of the residence nil rate band. Without the plan, her estate is £2.6 million, which is £600,000 over the £2 million line. Above that line, the residence nil rate band is reduced by £1 for every £2, under section 8D of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984, so she loses £300,000 of her £350,000. With the plan, the £500,000 debt brings her estate down to £2.1 million, and she loses only £50,000. This assumes she can claim her late husband's full unused nil rate band and residence nil rate band, the home goes to her children, she pays the interest and spends the annuity income, and the one-year guarantee has run out. If she saves the income instead, her estate grows again. One trap: the tax-free capital part of a purchased life annuity is not income for the normal expenditure out of income gift exemption, under section 21(3) of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984.

Does paying less inheritance tax mean more for the family?

No. In this example the family inherit about £200,000 less, because the £500,000 has gone to the annuity provider. Of that £500,000, the family bear £200,000, and the estate pays £300,000 less inheritance tax. The tax saving is a side effect of HIP 2.0. Its main job is to give Mrs Miggins, 85 and in good health, £3,856 a month more to live on, to start with. Equity release on its own does little for inheritance tax: borrowing and keeping the cash leaves the estate the same size. That changes if the money is spent or given away, and most gifts to people come under the seven year rule.

Sources Law: Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.3A · Steve's analysis: The Mrs Miggins example: arithmetic on the stated assumptions

Who called inheritance tax a voluntary levy paid by those who distrust their heirs?

Roy Jenkins, in the House of Commons on 19 March 1986, in the debate on the 1986 Budget. That Budget took most lifetime gifts out of capital transfer tax, which he saw as a return to death duties. Of that tax he said: "If people die old, it is, broadly speaking, a voluntary levy paid by those who distrust their heirs more than they dislike the Inland Revenue." The words are in Hansard, volume 94, column 325. The Finance Act 1986 renamed capital transfer tax as inheritance tax.

Interest rates rose, the bonds did not deliver, and some people lost the roof over their heads. In the early 1990s, the regulators effectively stopped the sale of those bond plans. In December 1991 the industry set up Safe Home Income Plans, SHIP. SHIP became the Equity Release Council in 2012. The original home income plan was much simpler.

Borrow against the home, buy an annuity, pay the interest out of the annuity, and live on what is left. On 8 July 1992, the Treasury minister Anthony Nelson told the House of Commons it was "the only sort of scheme to receive encouragement through the tax system". That was the tax position in 1992. The old tax relief on the interest is not available for new loans today.

3:44What could £500,000 pay an 85-year-old?

What could £500,000 pay an 85-year-old? In an illustration from a UK annuity provider, dated 12 October 2026, £500,000 bought a woman aged 85 a purchased life annuity of £76,747.44 a year. That is £6,395.62 a month, level, single life, paid monthly, with payments guaranteed for at least one year. Most of each payment is her own money coming back to her, plus the insurer's promise to keep paying for as long as she lives.

Mrs Miggins is made up, but her numbers are real. She is 85, a widow, and she still lives in the family home, worth £2.5 million. She has £100,000 in savings, and very little income. Capital rich, income poor. She takes out a lifetime mortgage of £500,000, a fifth of what the house is worth, and the £500,000 buys that annuity.

5:07How is purchased life annuity income taxed?

How is purchased life annuity income taxed? Only a small part of each payment is taxed. Most of it counts as her own capital coming back, which is tax free under sections 717 to 724 of the Income Tax (Trading and Other Income) Act 2005. On the illustration, £6,198.17 of each monthly payment is tax-free capital, and £197.45 is taxed as savings income.

The provider deducts 20% from that taxable slice, which is £39.49, and pays her £6,356.13 a month. That is the tax deducted, which may not be her final tax bill. With very little other income, she may be able to claim some or all of it back. From 6 April 2027, the basic rate on savings income goes up to 22%.

Correction, 29 October 2026: Recorded on 24 October 2026, when the planned rate was 22% (Finance Act 2026, s.5). Budget 2026, on 28 October 2026, set the basic rate on savings income at 24% from 6 April 2027. On the illustration, that is about £8 a month more tax, not about £4, if all of the taxable slice is taxed at that rate.

If all of the taxable slice is taxed at that rate, that is about £4 a month more.

6:24Pay the lifetime mortgage interest, or let it roll up?

Should she pay the lifetime mortgage interest, or let it roll up? Paying the interest keeps the debt where it is. Letting it roll up gives her more to spend now, but the debt grows. Assume a fixed lifetime mortgage rate of 6% a year. On £500,000, that is £2,500 a month. Choice one. She pays the interest every month out of the annuity.

That leaves her £3,856.13 a month to start with, and the debt stays at £500,000. The mortgage must allow her to pay the interest this way. Choice two. She lets the interest roll up. She keeps the whole £6,356.13 a month, but the debt grows. Added up yearly, it is about £670,000 after five years, and about £895,000 after ten. Compound interest works against you here.

The younger you are, the longer it has to grow, and the more paying the interest matters.

7:38Who might HIP 2.0 not suit?

Who might HIP 2.0 not suit? HIP 2.0 is a niche plan. Four groups of people should think hard before going near it. First, anyone who would rather downsize. If downsizing is an option, it should be considered first. Second, anyone in poor health. Purchased life annuities are not offered on impaired life terms, so they do not pay more for poor health.

The one niche exception is a care fees annuity, which is bought to pay for care. If health is poor, the maths changes. Third, younger people. The younger you are, the less the annuity pays, so there is far less left once the interest is paid. Fourth, anyone worried about dying early. The annuity stops, apart from anything left of the guarantee, but the loan still has to be repaid from the house.

Also, extra income can reduce means-tested benefits, such as Pension Credit.

8:43What are the risks and costs?

What are the risks and costs? The plan cannot be undone. After the 30 day cancellation period, the annuity cannot be cashed in. And the mortgage and the annuity are two separate contracts, so cancelling the annuity does not cancel the mortgage. The income is level, and prices will rise. At 2.5% inflation a year, in ten years, £3,856 a month will only buy what about £3,000 buys today.

It costs money to set up: valuation, legal, advice and arrangement fees. And the mortgage rate matters. Every quarter of one percent on £500,000 is about £104 a month.

9:32How do care fees work with an annuity in England?

How do care fees work with a purchased life annuity in England? In England, when the council works out what you pay towards your care, annuity income usually counts in full. But there is a rule that few people know. If the annuity was bought with a loan secured on your home, and you pay the interest, the part of the annuity income that pays the interest can be ignored.

The rule is in the Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, and England's care charging regulations of 2014 carry it across. The conditions are strict. At least 90% of the loan must buy an annuity for life. The interest must actually be paid, so interest that rolls up does not count. When the loan was made, you, and any joint annuitant, must have been at least 65, or State Pension age if that was higher.

And you, or another annuitant, must still live in the home. Whether a plan qualifies depends on the exact terms of the mortgage and the annuity. For Mrs Miggins, her £100,000 of savings is over England's £23,250 upper capital limit, so she would pay her own care fees at first anyway. If she moves into a care home for good, the rule stops, and the loan is usually repaid from the sale of the house.

The annuity carries on paying, towards her fees. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own care charging rules.

11:23What protections apply?

What protections apply to the lifetime mortgage and the annuity? Lifetime mortgages that meet the Equity Release Council's standards come with four key protections. A fixed rate, or a capped rate, for the life of the loan. The right to live in your home for life, as long as it stays your main home and you keep to the loan terms. The right to move to another suitable home. And a no negative equity guarantee.

When the house is sold properly, the estate can never owe more than the sale proceeds. Independent legal advice is required before signing. For the annuity, if an eligible insurer fails, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme protects 100% of the annuity, with no upper limit.

12:16How could inheritance tax fall by £300,000?

How could HIP 2.0 reduce inheritance tax by £300,000? On these assumptions, inheritance tax falls from £760,000 to £460,000. That is £300,000 less. £200,000 comes from the smaller estate, and £100,000 comes from getting back part of the residence nil rate band. These are the assumptions. She can claim her late husband's full unused nil rate band and residence nil rate band. The home goes to her children.

She pays the interest and spends the annuity income. And the one-year guarantee has run out, so nothing is left to pay to her estate. If she saves the income instead, her estate grows again. Without the plan, her estate is £2.6 million. That is £600,000 over the £2 million line. Above that line, the residence nil rate band is reduced by £1 for every £2, under section 8D of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984.

She loses £300,000 of her £350,000. With the plan, the £500,000 debt brings her estate down to £2.1 million. She loses only £50,000. There is one trap to watch. The tax-free capital part of a purchased life annuity is not income for the normal expenditure out of income gift exemption. That is section 21(3) of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984.

14:09Does less inheritance tax mean more for the family?

Does paying less inheritance tax mean more for the family? No, in this example the family inherit about £200,000 less, because the £500,000 has gone to the annuity provider. Of that £500,000, the family bear £200,000, and the estate pays £300,000 less inheritance tax. The tax saving is a side effect of HIP 2.0.

Its main job is to give Mrs Miggins, 85 and in good health, £3,856 a month more to live on, to start with. Equity release on its own does little for inheritance tax. Borrowing and keeping the cash leaves the estate the same size. That changes if the money is spent or given away, and most gifts to people come under the seven year rule.

Tax rules can change, and how they apply depends on your own circumstances. This is education only. It is not advice.

15:20Roy Jenkins on inheritance tax

And as always, I will leave you with those now famous words from Roy Jenkins, truer now than when he first spoke them in 1986 in the House of Commons, when he was talking about inheritance tax, where he said it was a voluntary levy paid by those who distrust their heirs more than they dislike the Inland Revenue.

Source note, 24 October 2026: Hansard, House of Commons, 19 March 1986, volume 94, column 325, in the debate on the 1986 Budget. That Budget took most lifetime gifts out of capital transfer tax, which Roy Jenkins saw as a return to death duties, and the Finance Act 1986 renamed the tax inheritance tax. His words: "If people die old, it is, broadly speaking, a voluntary levy paid by those who distrust their heirs more than they dislike the Inland Revenue."
