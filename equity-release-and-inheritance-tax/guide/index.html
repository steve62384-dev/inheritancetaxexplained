Equity release and inheritance tax: every question answered

By Steve Hunt ACII TEP · Published 14 October 2026 · Last reviewed 14 October 2026 · 39 questions

This guide answers the questions people ask about using equity release to pay an income, and what it does to inheritance tax:

  • what the plan is, which Steve calls HIP 2.0, and how it differs from the home income plans of the 1980s;
  • the real numbers, from a dated annuity quotation;
  • how it changes the inheritance tax bill;
  • how it works with care fees;
  • the risks and the protections;
  • what happens if the interest is left to roll up.

It draws on Steve's LinkedIn article of 14 October 2026, his worked figures, the Acts and HMRC's published guidance. Each answer stands on its own and ends with its sources. Where an answer is Steve's own reading or arithmetic rather than settled law, it says so.

The worked example is Mrs Miggins. She is fictitious. The arithmetic is not. She is 85, a widow in good health, living in England in a family home worth £2.5 million, with £100,000 of savings and little income. The annuity figures come from a real illustration dated 12 October 2026. The 6% mortgage rate is an assumption. The tax is today's tax, and real figures depend on the person and the day.

The short answer

It can, but only as a side effect, and it costs the family money. A lifetime mortgage, a loan secured on the home, buys a purchased life annuity, which pays an income for life. In Mrs Miggins's case, £500,000 borrowed buys £6,356.13 a month after tax. If she pays the £2,500 monthly interest out of the annuity, she has £3,856.13 a month left over, for life, and the debt stays at £500,000.

The debt comes off her estate. With an estate of £2.6 million, that brings back most of the residence nil rate band she was losing above £2 million, and on today's rules her inheritance tax falls by about £300,000. But the £500,000 has gone to the annuity company, so her family still inherit about £200,000 less than if she had done nothing. Put simply, the family bear £200,000 and the taxman bears £300,000. It suits someone in good health who wants to stay at home and wants the income. It is not for everyone.

The answers

Can equity release help with inheritance tax?

It can, in one particular way, but that is a side effect rather than the point. Equity release on its own does very little for inheritance tax: the money raised is still in the estate unless it is spent or given away, and gifts bring in the seven year clock. Steve's example uses the money to buy a purchased life annuity, which turns the capital into an income for life and leaves nothing behind when she dies.

The mortgage debt then comes off her estate. For Mrs Miggins, whose estate is above £2 million, that also brings back most of her residence nil rate band. On today's rules and Steve's assumptions, her inheritance tax falls by about £300,000. Her family still inherit about £200,000 less, because the £500,000 has gone to the annuity company in exchange for her income. The real point is the income: what is it worth to her not to worry about the heating bill?

What is a home income plan?

In its original form, a home income plan was very simple:

  • borrow against the home;
  • use the money to buy an annuity;
  • pay the interest out of the annuity;
  • live on what is left.

On 8 July 1992 Anthony Nelson, the Economic Secretary to the Treasury, told the House of Commons that this kind of plan was "the only sort of scheme to receive encouragement through the tax system". New loans got tax relief on the interest, on the first £30,000, until March 1999. That relief has gone for new loans, but the rules on paying for care still recognise the plan today.

Sources Law: Income and Corporation Taxes Act 1988, s.365 (home income plan interest relief); Finance Act 1999, s.39 (no relief for new loans from 9 March 1999); Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, Sch. 9, para. 17 · Published source: Hansard, House of Commons, 8 July 1992, vol. 211, cols. 359 to 371: Home income plans (column 367, Anthony Nelson, Economic Secretary to the Treasury)

Didn't home income plans cause a scandal?

Yes, a different kind did. In the late 1980s people borrowed against their homes and the money went into investment bonds. Interest rates rose, the bonds did not deliver, and some people lost the roof over their heads. Those bond plans were banned around 1990, and in 1991 the industry set up Safe Home Income Plans, SHIP, which became the Equity Release Council in 2012.

Steve's view is that the concept was not that bad; it was the application, and the circumstances, that made it a scandal. The plan in this guide is the original one, with today's protections: no investment bond and no stock market, just a lifetime mortgage and an annuity paying a fixed income for life.

Sources Published source: Hansard, House of Commons, 8 July 1992, vol. 211, cols. 359 to 371: Home income plans (column 367, Anthony Nelson, Economic Secretary to the Treasury); Equity Release Council: professional standards and guarantees · Steve's experience: Steve Hunt, in UK financial services since 1980

What is a lifetime mortgage?

A lifetime mortgage is the main kind of equity release. It is a loan secured on your home, usually repaid when you die or move into care for good, normally from the sale of the house. You can pay the interest as you go, or let it roll up and be added to the loan.

Lifetime mortgages that meet the Equity Release Council's standards come with protections, including a rate fixed or capped for life, the right to stay in the home, and the no negative equity guarantee. Those are covered in question 32.

Sources Published source: Equity Release Council: professional standards and guarantees

What is a purchased life annuity, and how is it different from a pension annuity?

A purchased life annuity is bought with your own money, not with a pension. You pay an insurer a lump sum, and it pays you an income for the rest of your life. Because the money was yours to start with, most of each payment is treated as your own capital coming back, and only a small interest slice is taxed.

A pension annuity is bought with pension money, and all of its income is taxed. They are completely different products, under different tax rules, and should not be confused. Only two UK insurers offer purchased life annuities, in Steve's understanding as at October 2026.

Sources Law: Income Tax (Trading and Other Income) Act 2005, s.717; Income Tax (Trading and Other Income) Act 2005, s.422 · Steve's experience: Steve's market knowledge as at 12 October 2026

Who might this suit?

The people Steve has in mind are capital rich but income poor: older homeowners, typically in their 80s, with a valuable home, little income and not much other capital, who want to stay in the family home. Mrs Miggins is the example: a house worth £2.5 million, £100,000 of savings, and a struggle every month to pay the heating bill.

It works best for someone in good health, because the annuity pays for as long as she lives. It is an option, not the option, and it suits some people and not others.

Sources Steve's analysis: Inheritance Tax... Can Equity Release Really Help? (LinkedIn article, 14 October 2026)

Who does it not suit?

Steve lists four groups:

  • Anyone who would be happy to downsize. If downsizing is an option it should be considered first, although moving house in your 80s is a huge upheaval. She could also borrow less, or use a drawdown plan, which brings extra risk and conditions.
  • Anyone in poor health. Neither purchased life annuity provider gives better rates for poor health, so a short life means a poor result.
  • Younger people. The younger you are, the lower the annuity rate and the longer the interest has to be paid or rolled up.
  • Anyone who would mind the risk of dying early. The annuity stops when she dies, but the loan does not.

The questions on age, dying early and costs give the numbers.

Why is most of the annuity income tax free?

Because most of each payment is treated as her own money coming back. The law splits each payment of a purchased life annuity into a capital part, which is exempt from income tax, and an interest part, which is taxed. On the insurer's figures, £6,198.17 of each £6,395.62 monthly payment is tax free and only £197.45 is taxed.

The insurer needs a form back from her (form PLA6) before it can pay the tax free part without tax. Until then it taxes the whole payment.

Sources Law: Income Tax (Trading and Other Income) Act 2005, s.717 · HMRC: HMRC Insurance Policyholder Taxation Manual, IPTM4360 (form PLA6); HMRC form PLA6: exemption from income tax on the capital element of a purchased life annuity · Provider evidence: Aviva Immediate Life Annuity illustration, 12 October 2026: £500,000, woman aged 85, single life, level, monthly in arrears, payments for at least a year, guaranteed to 21 November 2026

How is the rest taxed, and what changes in April 2027?

The taxed slice is savings income. The insurer must take 20% off it before paying her: 20% of £197.45 is £39.49 a month. If she pays no tax at all, she can ask to be paid gross on HMRC form R89. Depending on her other income and allowances, she may pay less tax, or none. The tax taken off is not necessarily her final bill.

Budget 2026, on 28 October 2026, set the basic rate on savings income at 24% from 6 April 2027. On her £197.45 slice, that is about £8 a month more tax.

What does the lifetime mortgage cost?

The one number Steve had to assume is the rate. He used 6% a year to keep the sums simple; the lowest rates he could see on 12 October 2026 were a little higher, at around 6.25%. On £500,000, 6% is £30,000 a year, or £2,500 a month. Every quarter of one per cent on £500,000 is about £104 a month.

There are also set-up costs: valuation, legal, advice and arrangement fees. Some of these fees can be waived by the lender, and some can be added to the loan.

How much is left if she pays the interest?

£6,356.13 comes in each month and £2,500 goes out, so she has £3,856.13 a month left over, for life. As long as she keeps paying the interest and no fees are added, the debt stays at £500,000.

At 85 there is plenty of room. The mortgage rate would have to be above about 15% before her annuity stopped covering the interest.

How does the plan reduce inheritance tax?

In two ways, both coming from the mortgage debt:

  • The debt comes off her estate. Inheritance tax is charged on what she owns less what she owes. The £500,000 she borrowed is still owed when she dies, and it is repaid from the house.
  • The annuity leaves nothing behind. It stops when she dies, so the £500,000 she paid for it is not in her estate either.

For most estates, that would save 40% of £500,000. For Mrs Miggins it saves more, because of the £2 million taper in the next question.

Sources Law: Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.5(3) and (5); Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.162; Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.175A · Steve's analysis: The Mrs Miggins example: arithmetic on the stated assumptions

What is the £2 million taper, and why does it matter here?

The residence nil rate band is an extra allowance of up to £175,000 when a home goes to children or grandchildren. A widow can usually add her late husband's unused amount, making up to £350,000. But for every £2 the estate is over £2 million, £1 of it is taken away. The estate is measured after debts.

So between £2 million and £2.7 million, every extra £1 in the estate costs 40p in tax plus 50p of lost allowance taxed at 40%: in effect 60p. Every £1 of debt in that zone saves the same 60p.

Sources Law: Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.8D; Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.8G; Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.8K · HMRC: HMRC Inheritance Tax Manual, IHTM46023 (the taper); HMRC Inheritance Tax Manual, IHTM46040 (the brought-forward allowance); HMRC guidance: the residence nil rate band

Do the family end up with more or less?

Less. Without the plan they would inherit £1,840,000 (£2,600,000 less £760,000 tax). With it they inherit £1,640,000 (£2,100,000 less £460,000 tax). That is £200,000 less, because the £500,000 has gone to the annuity company in exchange for her income.

Put simply, of that £500,000, the family bear £200,000 and the taxman bears £300,000. What the family get in return is a mother or grandmother with £3,856 a month to live on. Counting what she spends as well as what the family inherit, the plan can come out ahead if she lives long enough, as questions 35 to 37 show.

Can the mortgage really come off her estate?

Yes, on these facts. The law allows a debt to be deducted when:

  • it was incurred for full value, which a commercial loan from a lender is;
  • it is charged on the property, which reduces that property's value first;
  • it is actually repaid out of the estate after death, which a lifetime mortgage is, from the sale of the house.

A deduction is only as good as those conditions, so the loan must be a genuine commercial one, actually repaid.

Sources Law: Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.5(3) and (5); Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.162; Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.175A

Is buying the annuity a gift?

No, not on ordinary commercial terms. She pays an insurer the market price and gets an income for life in return, so her estate is not reduced when she buys it, and there is no intention to give anyone anything. When she dies the income stops, and there is nothing left to tax. Steve's reading is that this is a purchase, not a transfer of value.

Sources Law: Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.3; Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.10 · Steve's analysis: The Mrs Miggins example: arithmetic on the stated assumptions

Can she give the spare income to her family?

Not easily. There is a trap. The tax free capital part of a purchased life annuity does not count as income for the "normal expenditure out of income" gift exemption, so that exemption only helps with the small taxed slice.

Apart from the usual exemptions (her £3,000 a year, small gifts of up to £250 a person, and wedding gifts), gifts to the children start the seven year clock. At 85 that clock may well not run out. The plan works because she spends the money on herself.

Giving money away to avoid care fees is a different matter altogether. There is no seven year clock there. In fact there is no clock at all.

Sources Law: Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.21(1), (3) and (4); Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.19; Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.20; Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.22; Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.3A; Inheritance Tax Act 1984, s.7; Care and Support (Charging and Assessment of Resources) Regulations 2014, regs. 17 and 22

How does the annuity count if she needs care?

When a council works out what someone must pay towards care, income from an annuity normally counts in full. Her savings, and later what is left from the house, are counted under separate capital rules. In England, until her capital falls below £23,250, she pays the full cost of her care anyway.

Sources Law: Care and Support (Charging and Assessment of Resources) Regulations 2014, reg. 16 · Published source: Department of Health and Social Care: social care charging for care and support 2026 to 2027, local authority circular (upper capital limit £23,250)

What is the home income plan care fees rule?

If the annuity was bought with a loan secured on the home, the slice of annuity income that pays the interest is ignored when a council works out what someone pays towards care. That rule has been in the Income Support regulations since 1987, and the 2014 care charging rules carry it across. The conditions are:

  • at least 90% of the loan went into the annuity;
  • the interest is actually paid;
  • the borrower was at least 65, or State Pension age if higher, when the loan was made;
  • the loan is secured on her own home;
  • she, or the other person on the annuity, still lives there.

For Mrs Miggins herself, it only bites for care at home, once her savings have run down.

Sources Law: Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, Sch. 9, para. 17; Care and Support (Charging and Assessment of Resources) Regulations 2014, Sch. 1, para. 20; Care and Support (Charging and Assessment of Resources) Regulations 2014, reg. 16

Does rolling up the interest affect care fees?

Yes. The care fees rule only ignores interest that is actually paid. If the interest is left to roll up, nothing is ignored, and the whole annuity counts as income. That is one reason Steve says paying the interest matters for care fees, even at 85.

Sources Law: Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, Sch. 9, para. 17; Care and Support (Charging and Assessment of Resources) Regulations 2014, Sch. 1, para. 20

What happens if she moves into a care home for good?

The house is no longer her home, so the care fees rule stops. The lender will want the loan repaid, so the house will usually be sold, unless the family repay the loan another way. Under the Equity Release Council's standards there is no early repayment charge when she moves into care for good, on a doctor's certificate. The annuity carries on paying, every month for the rest of her life, towards her fees.

Sources Law: Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, Sch. 9, para. 17 · Published source: Equity Release Council: professional standards and guarantees · Steve's analysis: Inheritance Tax... Can Equity Release Really Help? (LinkedIn article, 14 October 2026)

What about a couple?

It is different. On a joint life annuity, if one of them moves into a care home and the other stays at home, the care fees rule carries on, because the other person on the annuity still lives there. But the numbers are tighter for younger couples, as question 14 shows, and a couple needs its own figures.

Sources Law: Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, Sch. 9, para. 17 · Provider evidence: Aviva quotation, 9 October 2026: £1 million, joint life, man aged 75 and woman aged 74, 100% to the survivor, scaled to £500,000

Does the income affect Pension Credit?

It can. Pension Credit counts income from an annuity, so the extra income can reduce Pension Credit or stop it.

The same rule as for care fees helps here. If she pays the interest, the slice of annuity income that pays the interest is ignored. The conditions are the same as for the care fees rule in question 23, with one difference: for Pension Credit she must have been at least 65, or State Pension age if that was higher, when the loan was made.

The Mrs Miggins figures leave means-tested benefits out. Anyone who gets them, or may soon need them, should have that checked before going ahead.

Sources Law: State Pension Credit Act 2002, s.15(1)(d) (income from annuity contracts counts as income); State Pension Credit Regulations 2002, Sch. IV, para. 10 (annuity bought with a loan secured on the home: the interest is ignored); Income Support (General) Regulations 1987, Sch. 9, para. 17

What if she dies early?

The annuity stops; the loan does not. The insurer pays for at least a year. But if Mrs Miggins died in year two, she would have had two years of income, and her family would still repay £500,000 from the house, more if the interest had been rolling up. Extra protection can be bought, but every pound of protection costs income.

On average, a woman of 85 lives about 6.9 more years, and that average includes people in poor health. Even so, it takes nearer eleven years of spare income to get the £500,000 back.

Sources Provider evidence: Aviva Immediate Life Annuity illustration, 12 October 2026: £500,000, woman aged 85, single life, level, monthly in arrears, payments for at least a year, guaranteed to 21 November 2026 · Published source: ONS: National life tables, life expectancy in England and Wales, 2021 to 2023 (a woman of 85: 6.86 years) · Steve's analysis: The Mrs Miggins example: arithmetic on the stated assumptions

Can she change her mind?

Only at the start. There is a 30 day cooling off period on the annuity. After that it cannot be cashed in: the insurer's illustration says so in black and white. The £500,000 has gone for good, in exchange for the income.

The lifetime mortgage can be paid off early, but there may be an early repayment charge, and these charges can be expensive. Some plans have none, some charge only for a set number of years, and some charge for the life of the plan. Under the FCA's rules, the charge must be a reasonable estimate of what early repayment costs the lender, and the lender must show the most she could have to pay before she signs.

Cancelling the annuity does not cancel the mortgage. That is one reason Steve keeps her £100,000 of savings where it is, as her rainy day money.

Sources Provider evidence: Aviva Immediate Life Annuity illustration, 12 October 2026: £500,000, woman aged 85, single life, level, monthly in arrears, payments for at least a year, guaranteed to 21 November 2026 · Published source: FCA Handbook, COBS 15.2 (the right to cancel, 30 days for a life policy); Equity Release Council: what happens if I want to repay the loan early?; FCA Handbook, MCOB 12.3 (early repayment charges: a reasonable pre-estimate of costs, and the maximum shown before the contract)

Does the income keep up with prices?

No. The income is level, and prices will rise. At 2.5% a year, in ten years £3,856 will only buy what about £3,000 buys today.

Sources Provider evidence: Aviva Immediate Life Annuity illustration, 12 October 2026: £500,000, woman aged 85, single life, level, monthly in arrears, payments for at least a year, guaranteed to 21 November 2026 · Steve's analysis: The Mrs Miggins example: arithmetic on the stated assumptions

Is it safe to pay the interest from the annuity?

Steve would want the interest payments to be voluntary, not compulsory. Then if she ever needs to stop paying, the interest simply rolls up, and the house is not put at risk. On a lifetime mortgage that meets the Council's standards the rate is fixed, or capped, for life, so the interest cannot run away from her.

Sources Published source: Equity Release Council: professional standards and guarantees · Steve's analysis: Inheritance Tax... Can Equity Release Really Help? (LinkedIn article, 14 October 2026)

What protections come with the lifetime mortgage?

Lifetime mortgages that meet the Equity Release Council's standards give these protections:

  • a rate fixed, or capped, for life;
  • the right to stay in the home for life, or until a move into care for good, as long as the loan terms are kept;
  • the right to move home, subject to the lender's criteria;
  • the no negative equity guarantee: if the house is sold properly and the loan terms are kept, the family can never owe more than the house is worth;
  • payments without penalty, within the lender's rules;
  • independent legal advice before she signs.

Sources Published source: Equity Release Council: professional standards and guarantees

What if the annuity company goes bust, and who can advise?

If the insurer failed, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme protects long-term insurance, which includes annuities, at 100%.

Equity release advice can only be given by, or through, an FCA authorised firm, by an adviser who holds the equity release qualification. The annuity needs an adviser qualified for that as well.

Sources Law: Financial Services and Markets Act 2000, s.19 · Published source: Financial Services Compensation Scheme: what we cover (long-term insurance 100% protected)

What is HIP 2.0?

HIP 2.0 is Steve's name for the plan in this guide: the original home income plan, with today's protections. A lifetime mortgage buys a purchased life annuity, and in the main example Mrs Miggins pays the interest out of the annuity.

This part of the guide looks at a variation: HIP 2.0 with the interest rolled up. Instead of paying the interest, Mrs Miggins lets it roll up and spends the whole annuity, £6,356.13 a month. The debt grows, so her estate shrinks faster, and while her estate is in the £2 million taper zone each extra £1 of debt saves up to 60p of inheritance tax. Steve's idea is to review it every five years.

It is Steve's own analysis, not a product. It works only if she actually spends the money on herself: if she saves it, it stays in her estate. The next five questions give the figures, on what Steve calls an ultra cautious basis.

Does rolling up change the care fees and the risks?

Yes:

  • the home income plan care fees rule is lost, because no interest is paid;
  • the debt grows with interest on interest, so the family's share of the house shrinks faster;
  • the no negative equity guarantee still applies, so the family can never owe more than the house is worth;
  • if she does not spend the extra income, there is no inheritance tax benefit, because it stays in her estate.

That is why Steve's idea includes a review every five years.

Can the HIP 2.0 figures be used for someone else?

Not without new figures. The annuity rate depends on age, so a person of 75 needs a new quotation, and the interest may be harder to cover. A couple needs a joint life annuity and a two-life model, because the first death usually passes everything to the survivor free of inheritance tax. Steve's model is for one person, in good health, on these figures.
